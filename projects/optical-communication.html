Single-Photon Optical Communication

Ongoing

Free-space optical communication in the photon-starved regime — where the receiver counts individual photons rather than measuring a continuous optical power. Detecting single photons is what makes an optical link work across distances and power budgets where a conventional receiver would see nothing at all, which is why it underpins deep-space optical communication and quantum key distribution (QKD).

The project works from a published drone-based QKD experiment (arXiv:2505.17587), partnered with a national research lab and another university team, over an ~18-month runway. The long-term goal is a link where one terminal flies on a drone.

Why it has to be so precise

When the signal is counted one photon at a time, there is no margin to waste. A conventional receiver can afford to lose a fraction of a bright beam; a single-photon link cannot, because every photon that misses the detector is information that never arrives. The light has to be steered onto a very small target — a detector’s active area or the core of a single-mode fiber, only a few micrometres across — and held there.

  • Pointing is the link budget. Over a free-space path, a tilt error of a few microradians walks the beam off the receiver. Holding the terminal on target matters as much as the detector itself.
  • Every disturbance counts. Vibration, thermal drift and air turbulence all move the beam; at single-photon rates there is no excess signal to average them away, so they have to be measured and corrected continuously.
  • Background is the enemy. Stray light produces detector counts that look exactly like signal photons, so the optics, filtering and timing all have to keep the background far below the true signal.

The photon source

The quantum source starts with a 405 nm laser aimed into a nonlinear crystal. Inside it, a single 405 nm photon is converted into a pair of entangled photons at 810 nm — twice the wavelength, each carrying half the energy (spontaneous parametric down-conversion). The two photons leave the crystal at about 30° to either side of the pump beam, one to the left and one to the right, so each arm has to be aligned to catch photons coming off at that exact angle.

The bench proof of concept

Before any entangled photons, the link itself is proven with two attenuated 850 nm lasers sharing one fiber:

  1. Two identical fiber-pigtailed 850 nm lasers are merged by a 99:1 fiber combiner — a bright clock channel (99%) and a faint data channel (1%).
  2. The transmit fast steering mirror sends the beam across free space to the receiver.
  3. The receive FSM catches it; an 8% pickoff feeds the fine-pointing sensor and the other 92% goes to the detection chain.
  4. An FPGA recovers the timing from the clock pulses, decodes the data and measures the bit error rate.

Data uses binary pulse-position modulation: an early pulse in a time slot is one bit value, a late pulse the other. With one clock pulse per N data frames of length T, the data rate is R = N / ((N+1) T).

Pointing, acquisition and tracking

Pointing is three-stage and symmetric — every stage exists on both terminals, and each only has to land the beam inside the next stage’s capture range.

MediumNIR camera tracking a four-diode IR beacon on the far terminal → gimbal: pose from the distortion of the pattern. Works in the dark, where printed markers fail.
FineQuadrant photodiode behind the 8% pickoff → fast steering mirror: closed-loop fine track
  • The gimbal’s job is to land the beam inside the FSM’s capture range; the FSM does the microradian work.
  • Tracking bandwidth is set by the vision chain — centroid accuracy, latency and frame rate — not by encoder resolution.

The gimbal (my technical focus)

Our terminal is a two-axis direct-drive gimbal: RLS M8325s motors on two ODrive S1 controllers, coordinated by a Teensy 4.1 (see the gimbal). The partner team’s terminal runs its own controller, so a common angle-in / angle-out command interface is defined before the acquisition state machine is written.

  • The onboard 12-bit encoder resolves 2π/212 ≈ 1.53 mrad per count — enough for the coarse role, because the outer loop closes on the camera and corrects absolute encoder error away.
  • The real risk is slop the motor-side encoder can’t see. The plan adds 14-bit load-side encoders if the pitch-axis rebuild measurement shows load-side error.

Running the project

The build is organised into three workstreams — gimbal mechanical, pointing and controls software, and optical bench and comms — each with a lead, a bill of materials and a task list tied to one Gantt chart. The critical path runs through optics procurement, not the gimbals.
