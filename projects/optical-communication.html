Single-Photon Optical Communication

Ongoing

Free-space optical communication in the photon-starved regime — where the receiver counts individual photons rather than measuring a continuous optical power. Detecting single photons is what makes an optical link work across distances and power budgets where a conventional receiver would see nothing at all, which is why it underpins deep-space optical communication.

Why it has to be so precise

When the signal is counted one photon at a time, there is no margin to waste. A conventional receiver can afford to lose a fraction of a bright beam; a single-photon link cannot, because every photon that misses the detector is information that never arrives. The light has to be steered onto a very small target — a detector’s active area or the core of a single-mode fiber, only a few micrometres across — and held there.

  • Pointing is the link budget. Over a free-space path, a tilt error of a few microradians walks the beam off the receiver. Holding the terminal on target matters as much as the detector itself.
  • Every disturbance counts. Vibration, thermal drift and air turbulence all move the beam; at single-photon rates there is no excess signal to average them away, so they have to be measured and corrected continuously.
  • Background is the enemy. Stray light produces detector counts that look exactly like signal photons, so the optics, filtering and timing all have to keep the background far below the true signal.

The optical setup

The source starts with a 405 nm laser. In a nonlinear crystal, a 405 nm photon can split into two photons at twice the wavelength, 810 nm — energy is conserved, so each carries half the energy (spontaneous parametric down-conversion). The result is a source of single 810 nm photons produced in correlated pairs, which is what the link detects and counts.

  • Fast steering mirrors (FSMs) do the fine pointing: small mirrors that tilt in microradian steps at high bandwidth to cancel jitter and drift faster than any motor stage could.
  • A two-axis gimbal does the coarse pointing — large, slow moves that bring the beam into the FSMs’ small correction range. That is the platform I am building for this work: direct drive, with no gearbox backlash in the loop (see the gimbal).
  • Coarse and fine stages together form a nested pointing loop, the standard architecture for keeping a narrow beam locked on target.
